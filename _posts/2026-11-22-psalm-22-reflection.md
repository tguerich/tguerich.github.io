A Reflection on Psalm 22
{: .study-subtitle}

Tony Guerich · November 22, 2026
{: .study-byline}

I woke up right at dawn, in a cheerful, silly mood.

Then I opened Psalm 22.

The heading is:

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>For the choirmaster. To the tune of “The Doe of the Dawn.” A Psalm of David.</em></p>

I woke at dawn, and the psalm was set to the Doe of the Dawn.

And the first line sobered me right up.

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>My God, my God,</em><br />
<em>why have You forsaken me?</em><br />
Psalm 22:1 (BSB)</p>

Those are the words Jesus cried from the cross.

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>About the ninth hour Jesus cried out in a loud voice, “Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani?” which means, “My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?”</em><br />
Matthew 27:46 (BSB)</p>

There's no getting past that cheerfully.

But this morning I kept reading, all the way to the last verse.

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>They will come and proclaim His righteousness</em><br />
<em>to a people yet unborn —</em><br />
<em>all that He has done.</em><br />
Psalm 22:31 (BSB)</p>

He has done it.

What?

The Hebrew ends on two words, with no object: ki 'asah, "for He has done it."<sup id="fnref1"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup>

It does not say what He did. Only that He did it. The object is left open on purpose.

I think the implication is everything He intended to. Everything.

The old Greek translation spells it out: "that the Lord has done it."

And it renders "a people yet unborn" as "a people that will be born."

That generation was not here yet. That takes in us.

As Brian Roemmele says, you need to read things backwards to really understand them.

We always start this psalm with verse 1.

But that was the means, not the ends.

Read from the end, the forsakenness was the road, and "He has done it" was the purpose all along.

The psalm is multi-level.

On one level it is David's own cry in his own trouble. That trouble was real.

On another it reads like an eyewitness at the cross: the mocking, the thirst, the garments divided, the hands and the feet.

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>All who see me mock me;</em><br />
<em>they sneer and shake their heads.</em><br />
Psalm 22:7 (BSB)</p>

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>My tongue sticks to the roof of my mouth.</em><br />
Psalm 22:15 (BSB)</p>

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>They have pierced my hands and feet.</em><br />
Psalm 22:16 (BSB)<sup id="fnref2"><a href="#fn2">2</a></sup></p>

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>They divide my garments among them</em><br />
<em>and cast lots for my clothing.</em><br />
Psalm 22:18 (BSB)</p>

And it reaches all the way out, to the nations and to a people yet unborn.

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>All the ends of the earth</em><br />
<em>will remember and turn to the LORD.</em><br />
<em>All the families of the nations</em><br />
<em>will bow down before Him.</em><br />
Psalm 22:27 (BSB)</p>

That is where "Joy to the World" belongs, as it was originally intended.

Isaac Watts did not write it as a Christmas carol.

In 1719, in *The Psalms of David Imitated*, he wrote it as a paraphrase of Psalm 98, under the heading "The Messiah's Coming and Kingdom."

It is about the Lord coming to reign.<sup id="fnref3"><a href="#fn3">3</a></sup>

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>He rules the world with truth and grace,</em><br />
<em>And makes the nations prove</em><br />
<em>The glories of his righteousness,</em><br />
<em>And wonders of his love.</em></p>

As it was first meant, the hymn sings what the psalm is aiming at: the King has come, His work is done, and the nations hear it and rejoice.

His righteousness. The last verse of the psalm says they will come and proclaim it, to a people yet unborn.

Many hear Jesus' words from the cross, "It is finished" (John 19:30, BSB), in that ending.

That is a devotional echo, not a word link. John uses a different verb.<sup id="fnref4"><a href="#fn4">4</a></sup>

But the shape is the same. The psalm that begins where the cross begins ends with the work done.

A people yet unborn includes us, readers today, on every side of the world.

The psalm that opened with "My God, my God, why have You forsaken me?" closes on two words.

He has done it.

## Notes

<sup id="fn1"><a href="#fnref1">1</a></sup> The Hebrew text is from the Miqra according to the Masorah on Sefaria. English quotations are from the Berean Standard Bible, via bible.helloao.org. In the Hebrew numbering, the title is verse 1, so this line stands in verse 32; in English it is verse 31. The Hebrew of the verse is:<br />
<span lang="he" dir="rtl" style="display:block; margin:0.4em 0;">יָ֭בֹאוּ וְיַגִּ֣ידוּ צִדְקָת֑וֹ לְעַ֥ם נ֝וֹלָ֗ד כִּ֣י עָשָֽׂה׃</span> It ends <span lang="he" dir="rtl">כִּ֣י עָשָֽׂה</span>, ki 'asah, "for He has done it." The verb <span lang="he">עָשָׂה</span> ('asah) means "he has done" or "he has made," and it has no object here. The Greek Old Testament (Rahlfs, 1935) reads <span lang="grc">ὅτι ἐποίησεν ὁ κύριος</span>, "that the Lord has done it," and renders "a people yet unborn" as <span lang="grc">λαῷ τῷ τεχθησομένῳ</span>, "to a people that will be born." In that numbering this is Psalm 21:32.

<sup id="fn2"><a href="#fnref2">2</a></sup> Psalm 22:16, BSB, follows the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Greek Old Testament, the Vulgate, and the Syriac: "they have pierced my hands and feet." Most Masoretic manuscripts read "like a lion at my hands and feet."

<sup id="fn3"><a href="#fnref3">3</a></sup> Isaac Watts published "Joy to the World" in *The Psalms of David Imitated* (1719) as the second part of his paraphrase of Psalm 98, under the heading "The Messiah's Coming and Kingdom." It was not written as a Christmas carol; that association came later. See Hymnary.org, "Joy to the World," https://hymnary.org/text/joy_to_the_world_the_lord_is_come, drawing on the Psalter Hymnal Handbook and John Julian, *Dictionary of Hymnology* (1907). The quoted stanza is in the public domain.

<sup id="fn4"><a href="#fnref4">4</a></sup> John 19:30, BSB: "It is finished." The verb in the Greek New Testament (SBLGNT) is <span lang="grc">τετέλεσται</span> (tetelestai), "it has been completed," from <span lang="grc">τελέω</span>, "to finish or complete." The last two words of Psalm 22 in Hebrew use a different verb, <span lang="he">עָשָׂה</span>, "he has done," and the Greek Old Testament uses <span lang="grc">ἐποίησεν</span>, "he has done." The connection is in the shape of the psalm, not in a shared word.
