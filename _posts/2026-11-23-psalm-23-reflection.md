A Reflection on Psalm 23
{: .study-subtitle}

Tony Guerich · November 23, 2026
{: .study-byline}

The line is so familiar that we can almost miss it.

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>Even though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death,</em><br />
<em>I will fear no evil,</em><br />
<em>for You are with me;</em><br />
<em>Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me.</em><br />
Psalm 23:4 (BSB)</p>

Not death, but its shadow.

The Hebrew word tsalmavet was pointed as two words joined into one: tsel, “shadow,” and mavet, “death.”<sup id="fnref1"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup>

The old Greek translators read it the same way: “in the midst of the shadow of death.”

Some English versions render it “deep darkness.” The sense is close either way.

David does not say he walks into death.

He walks through its valley, and only the shadow falls on him.

“I will fear no evil, for You are with me.”

A shadow cannot fall unless there is a light.

Isaiah saw it long ago.

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>The people walking in darkness have seen a great light;</em><br />
<em>on those living in the land of the shadow of death,</em><br />
<em>a light has dawned.</em><br />
Isaiah 9:2 (BSB)</p>

Matthew hears that promise come to rest in Jesus.

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>the people living in darkness have seen a great light;</em><br />
<em>on those living in the land of the shadow of death,</em><br />
<em>a light has dawned.</em><br />
Matthew 4:16 (BSB)</p>

Where there is a shadow, a Light has come.

Paul takes the thought one step further, and he sings it as a taunt.

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>“Where, O Death, is your victory?</em><br />
<em>Where, O Death, is your sting?”</em><br />
1 Corinthians 15:55 (BSB)</p>

He is quoting Hosea, mostly as it stood in the old Greek.<sup id="fnref2"><a href="#fn2">2</a></sup>

The sting is drawn. Death is real, but it is not the last word.

Only the shadow is left.

And the comfort comes from the Shepherd.

“Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me.”

The rod protects. The staff supports. Both are in His hand.

Jesus says it this way.

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>My sheep listen to My voice; I know them, and they follow Me. I give them eternal life, and they will never perish. No one can snatch them out of My hand.</em><br />
John 10:27–28 (BSB)</p>

The staff that comforts in the valley becomes the hand no one can snatch us out of.

“They will never perish” is as strong as the Greek can make it, a doubled “no.”<sup id="fnref3"><a href="#fn3">3</a></sup>

He can promise that because He did not stop at the shadow.

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down His life for the sheep.</em><br />
John 10:11 (BSB)</p>

He went through death itself, so for His sheep only the shadow is left.

The story is told of Donald Grey Barnhouse, driving with his children to his wife’s funeral, when a truck passed and its shadow swept over the car. He asked them whether they would rather be run over by the truck or by its shadow. Then he said the truck ran over Jesus, and only the shadow passes over us.<sup id="fnref4"><a href="#fn4">4</a></sup>

That is the psalm’s comfort, and it is the gospel’s.

Bill and Gloria Gaither gave that comfort a name many of us know: “Because He Lives.”<sup id="fnref5"><a href="#fn5">5</a></sup>

The title is enough.

The Shepherd went through death itself.

He lives.

And only the shadow falls.

## Notes

<sup id="fn1"><a href="#fnref1">1</a></sup> The Hebrew text is from the Miqra according to the Masorah on Sefaria. English quotations are from the Berean Standard Bible, via bible.helloao.org. The word is <span lang="he">צַלְמָוֶת</span>, tsalmavet. As pointed by the Masoretes it reads as a compound of <span lang="he">צֵל</span> (tsel), “shadow,” and <span lang="he">מָוֶת</span> (mavet), “death.” Brown-Driver-Briggs (p. 853) prefers this reading, noting that the Greek Old Testament usually has <span lang="grc">σκιὰ θανάτου</span>, “shadow of death.” Other scholars would re-point the word as tsalmut, “deep darkness.” The BSB footnote at Psalm 23:4 gives both: “Or the valley of deep darkness.” The old Greek of this verse (Rahlfs, 1935, Psalm 22:4 in that numbering) reads <span lang="grc">ἐν μέσῳ σκιᾶς θανάτου</span>, “in the midst of the shadow of death.”

<sup id="fn2"><a href="#fnref2">2</a></sup> 1 Corinthians 15:55, BSB. Paul is citing Hosea 13:14, mostly from the old Greek (Rahlfs): <span lang="grc">ποῦ ἡ δίκη σου θάνατε ποῦ τὸ κέντρον σου ᾅδη</span>, “Where is your penalty, O Death? Where is your sting, O Hades?” The Greek New Testament (SBLGNT) reads <span lang="grc">ποῦ σου, θάνατε, τὸ νῖκος; ποῦ σου, θάνατε, τὸ κέντρον;</span>, “Where, O Death, is your victory? Where, O Death, is your sting?” Paul follows the Greek shape of Hosea and keeps “sting,” but writes “victory” where the Greek of Hosea has “penalty,” and he addresses Death twice, where Hosea addresses Death and then Sheol (Hades in the Greek).

<sup id="fn3"><a href="#fnref3">3</a></sup> John 10:28, BSB: “they will never perish.” The Greek (SBLGNT) is <span lang="grc">οὐ μὴ ἀπόλωνται εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα</span>, a doubled negative: “they will not perish, not ever.”

<sup id="fn4"><a href="#fnref4">4</a></sup> The illustration is widely attributed to Donald Grey Barnhouse, after the death of his first wife. I have not been able to pin it to a page in a book of his. A common retelling is in Billy Graham, *Facing Death*. Details vary from telling to telling, so it is offered here as “the story is told.”

<sup id="fn5"><a href="#fnref5">5</a></sup> Bill and Gloria Gaither, “Because He Lives” (1971). The song remains under copyright; only the title is quoted here.
