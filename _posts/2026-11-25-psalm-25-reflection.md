A Reflection on Psalm 25
{: .study-subtitle}

Tony Guerich · November 25, 2026
{: .study-byline}

The psalm turns, and David says it plainly.

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>Turn to me and be gracious,</em><br />
<em>for I am lonely and afflicted.</em><br />
Psalm 25:16 (BSB)</p>

The Hebrew word is yachid: lonely, only, one of a kind.<sup id="fnref1"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup>

The first thing God called “not good” was a man alone.

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>The LORD God also said, “It is not good for the man to be alone. I will make for him a suitable helper.”</em><br />
Genesis 2:18 (BSB)</p>

Adam’s word there is levado, “by himself,” not yachid. The words are different, but the wound is the same.

It was not good then. It is not good now.

Follow yachid through the book, and it never once lands on God.<sup id="fnref2"><a href="#fn2">2</a></sup>

Twelve times in all.

It is Isaac, “your only son,” whom Abraham loved, on the way to Moriah.

It is Jephthah’s only daughter, coming out to meet him with tambourines.

It is the mourning “as for an only child,” and Zechariah’s line, “they will look on Me, the One they have pierced.”

It is “my precious life” in Psalm 22, the psalm of the cross.

And it is the lonely, here, asking God to turn and be gracious.

The Shema does not use that word of God.

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is One.</em><br />
Deuteronomy 6:4 (BSB)</p>

The word is echad, “one.” It is the same word used when a man and a woman “become one flesh.”

Echad can be a unity. Not a bare single.

God is one, but not yachid.

God was never alone. The Father, the Son, and the Spirit were already one, together.

Later Jewish prayer does use yachid of God. We should say so plainly.

Maimonides, in the twelfth century, set down thirteen principles of faith. The second is the unity of God, a oneness that cannot be divided. He still cited the Shema’s word, echad.

The prayer book went a step further. In the Ani Ma’amin, the “I believe” that follows those principles, the congregation says the Creator “is yachid,” unique, “and there is no uniqueness like His in any manner.”

The hymn Yigdal sings it too: “He is One, and there is no unique one like His uniqueness.”<sup id="fnref3"><a href="#fn3">3</a></sup>

That later use is serious, and it should be heard with respect.

But the book itself does not say it. In all twelve places, yachid is an only child, a solitary life, or a lonely man. Never God.

The honest work is exegesis, not eisegesis: to draw out what is in the text, not to read into it what is not there.

Jesus confronted this same tendency to read things into the text.

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>You pore over the Scriptures because you presume that by them you possess eternal life. These are the very words that testify about Me, yet you refuse to come to Me to have life.</em><br />
John 5:39–40 (BSB)</p>

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>If you had believed Moses, you would believe Me, because he wrote about Me.</em><br />
John 5:46 (BSB)</p>

Moses wrote about Him.

The old Greek translators heard two shades in the one Hebrew word.

Sometimes they rendered it monogenes, “only, unique.” That is the word John’s Gospel uses of Jesus: “the one and only Son from the Father.”

Sometimes they rendered it agapetos, “beloved.” That is the word they used for Isaac, “your beloved son.” And that is the word the Father’s voice uses at the baptism: “You are My beloved Son.”<sup id="fnref4"><a href="#fn4">4</a></sup>

So the lonely man’s word becomes the Father’s word for His only, beloved Son.

The Son walked the road of Psalm 22. He was forsaken, so that the lonely would never be left alone.

And the same book that lets David say, “I am lonely,” also says this:

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>God settles the lonely in families.</em><br />
Psalm 68:6 (BSB)</p>

The reader of verse 16 is not alone.

God turns and is gracious.

He was never yachid.

And neither, in His care, are we.

## Notes

<sup id="fn1"><a href="#fnref1">1</a></sup> The Hebrew text is from the Miqra according to the Masorah on Sefaria. English quotations are from the Berean Standard Bible, via bible.helloao.org. Psalm 25:16 reads <span lang="he">פְּנֵה־אֵלַי וְחָנֵּנִי כִּי־יָחִיד וְעָנִי אָנִי</span>, “Turn to me and be gracious, for I am lonely and afflicted.” The word <span lang="he">יָחִיד</span>, yachid, is an adjective meaning “only,” “unique,” or, in this verse, “lonely.” In Genesis 2:18 the phrase is <span lang="he">לֹא־טוֹב הֱיוֹת הָאָדָם לְבַדּוֹ</span>, lo tov heyot ha’adam levado, “It is not good for the man to be alone.” The word there is <span lang="he">לְבַדּוֹ</span>, levado, “by himself,” not yachid.

<sup id="fn2"><a href="#fnref2">2</a></sup> According to the Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible (lemma 3173), yachid occurs twelve times: Genesis 22:2, 12, 16; Judges 11:34; Proverbs 4:3; Jeremiah 6:26; Amos 8:10; Zechariah 12:10; Psalm 22:20 (Hebrew 22:21); Psalm 25:16; Psalm 35:17; and Psalm 68:6 (Hebrew 68:7). In none of these is the word used of God. “My precious life” in Psalm 22:20 is, in the Hebrew, <span lang="he">יְחִידָתִי</span>, “my only one.” Zechariah 12:10, BSB: “they will look on Me, the One they have pierced. They will mourn for Him as one mourns for an only child.” The Shema, Deuteronomy 6:4, uses <span lang="he">אֶחָד</span>, echad, “one,” the same word as “one flesh” in Genesis 2:24.

<sup id="fn3"><a href="#fnref3">3</a></sup> Moses Maimonides (1138–1204) set down thirteen principles of faith in his Commentary on the Mishnah, Sanhedrin 10 (Perek Helek). The work was written in Judeo-Arabic; the Hebrew quoted here is the traditional translation, Vilna edition, on Sefaria: https://www.sefaria.org/Rambam_on_Mishnah_Sanhedrin.10.1. The second principle is “the unity (yichud) of God”: He is “one in a unity that has no unity like it,” and Maimonides points to the Shema, “the LORD is one” (echad). He does not, in that text, call God yachid. The later prayer-book paraphrase known as Ani Ma’amin, “I believe,” does: “I believe with complete faith that the Creator, blessed is His Name, is unique (yachid), and there is no uniqueness like His in any manner; and that He alone is our God, Who was, is, and will ever be.” Hebrew and English from the Metsudah Siddur (Avrohom Davis, 1981), Siddur Ashkenaz, Weekday, Shacharit, Post Service, Thirteen Principles 2, on Sefaria: https://www.sefaria.org/Siddur_Ashkenaz,_Weekday,_Shacharit,_Post_Service,_Thirteen_Principles.2. The hymn Yigdal, attributed to Daniel ben Yehudah, a judge in Rome about 1300, and based on those same principles, sings <span lang="he">אֶחָד וְאֵין יָחִיד כְּיִחוּדוֹ</span>, “He is One, and there is no unique one like His uniqueness.” Metsudah Siddur, Siddur Ashkenaz, Weekday, Shacharit, Preparatory Prayers, Yigdal, on Sefaria: https://www.sefaria.org/Siddur_Ashkenaz,_Weekday,_Shacharit,_Preparatory_Prayers,_Yigdal. That later language describes a oneness that cannot be divided, and it has often been heard in contrast to Christian teaching about the Father, the Son, and the Spirit. The Bible itself, however, never uses yachid of God.

<sup id="fn4"><a href="#fnref4">4</a></sup> The Greek Old Testament is Rahlfs (1935). It renders yachid as <span lang="grc">μονογενής</span>, monogenes, “only, unique,” at Judges 11:34 and at Psalms 22:20, 35:17, and 25:16 (LXX 21:21, 34:17, and 24:16). It renders the word as <span lang="grc">ἀγαπητός</span>, agapetos, “beloved,” at Genesis 22:2, 12, 16, and at Jeremiah 6:26, Amos 8:10, and Zechariah 12:10. (Psalm 68:6 is <span lang="grc">μονοτρόπους</span>, “solitary ones”; Proverbs 4:3 is paraphrased <span lang="grc">ἀγαπώμενος</span>, “loved.”) John 1:14 and 3:16, BSB, “one and only Son,” use <span lang="grc">μονογενής</span> in the Greek New Testament (SBLGNT): <span lang="grc">δόξαν ὡς μονογενοῦς παρὰ πατρός</span> and <span lang="grc">τὸν υἱὸν τὸν μονογενῆ</span>. Mark 1:11, BSB, “You are My beloved Son,” is <span lang="grc">Σὺ εἶ ὁ υἱός μου ὁ ἀγαπητός</span> (SBLGNT).
