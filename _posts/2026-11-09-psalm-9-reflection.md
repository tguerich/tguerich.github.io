*‘al-mut labben (a transliteration)*

That is how the title of Psalm 9 reads in Hebrew, and it is where I want to begin. Word for word, it sounds like “upon the death of the son.” But its meaning is uncertain; it may be the name of a tune or a musical direction. The World English Bible renders it this way:

*For the Chief Musician. Set to “The Death of the Son.” A Psalm by David.*  
Psalm 9, title (WEB)

Whatever it first meant, the phrase is sad.

It reminds me of 2 Samuel 12. After Bathsheba’s first son died, David “arose from the earth, and washed and anointed himself, and changed his clothing; and he came into Yahweh’s house, and worshiped” (2 Samuel 12:20, WEB).

I need to be honest: the title does not prove this psalm was written that day. The connection may be direct motivation, inspiration, or illustration. But that is what poetry does. It reminds us. It causes reflection. It moves us.

This psalm also reminds me not to overlook the title. In Hebrew, the title is verse 1. It is part of the text, not a label to skip. And right after it comes this:

*I will give thanks to Yahweh with my whole heart.*  
*I will tell of all your marvelous works.*  
*I will be glad and rejoice in you.*  
*I will sing praise to your name, O Most High.*  
Psalm 9:1–2 (WEB)

A heavy title, then whole-hearted thanks. The psalm does not hide the sorrow; it sits in the very first verse. But sorrow is not the end of the song. Grief leads into worship: giving thanks, telling of God’s wonders, singing praise to the name of the Most High.

That does not mean we stop grieving. David wept and fasted first. It means grief does not get the last word.

*Lord Most High, you know the sorrows we carry. Keep us from skipping past them, and from being buried by them. Help us, like David, to rise, come into your presence, and give you thanks with our whole heart. Amen.*

**Notes**  
Scripture (English): World English Bible (WEB, public domain), eBible.org. Psalm 9: <https://ebible.org/eng-web/PSA009.htm>. 2 Samuel 12:20: <https://ebible.org/eng-web/2SA12.htm>.

Verse numbering: The Hebrew Bible counts the title as verse 1, so English Psalm 9:1–2 = Hebrew Psalm 9:2–3.

Hebrew: Sefaria, Miqra according to the Masorah (MAM), Psalms 9:1, <https://www.sefaria.org/Psalms.9.1>. The transliteration ‘al-mut labben is my own simplified one.

The title’s meaning: The JPS Tanakh (1985), on Sefaria, renders it ‘almuth labben and notes: “Meaning of Heb. uncertain; some mss. and ancient versions, ‘al muth labben, as though ‘over the death of the son.’” The Catholic Encyclopedia (“Psalms,” <https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/12533a.htm>) says it “probably means ‘set to the tune “Death Whitens”’,” and McClintock and Strong’s Cyclopedia (“Muthlabben,” <https://www.biblicalcyclopedia.com/M/muthlabben.html>) surveys several other conjectures.
