Psalm 13 is a short psalm, but it tells the truth about a long night. David's question is as old as grief, and as close as the next funeral.

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>How long, O LORD? Will You forget me forever? How long will You hide Your face from me?</em><br />
Psalm 13:1 (BSB)</p>

He is not asking for a lecture. He is asking whether God is still there, and how long the silence will last. Then comes the plea at the heart of the psalm:

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>Consider me and respond, O LORD my God.</em><br />
<em>Give light to my eyes, lest I sleep in death,</em><br />
Psalm 13:3 (BSB)</p>

In the Hebrew, that last line is:

<p dir="rtl" lang="he" style="font-size:1.4em">הָאִ֥ירָה עֵ֝ינַ֗י פֶּן־אִישַׁ֥ן הַמָּֽוֶת׃</p>

Word for word, it is something like, "Make my eyes shine, lest I sleep the death." David fears the lamp going out. If the light in his eyes fails, he sleeps the death.

I know that fear. As I get older I am increasingly aware of my mortality. From this world:

<p style="margin-left:36pt">My father is gone,<br />
My mother is gone,<br />
My brother is gone,<br />
My aunts and uncles are gone,<br />
Many friends are gone.</p>

I just remember Dad saying he was the "last of the Mohicans." Now I am the last of the Mohicans. So I can identify with David and his complaint.

The list is long enough to make a man ask "How long?" and wonder what will be left when the last light goes out. I have stood at enough gravesides to hear that question in my own mouth. David did not pretend the danger was small. Neither will I.

But the psalm does not end with the complaint, and neither does the gospel. Paul takes up that same fear and turns it. "Listen, I tell you a mystery," he writes: "We will not all sleep, but we will all be changed—" (1 Corinthians 15:51, BSB). For those who belong to Christ, **the death David dreaded has become sleep**. And sleep, by its nature, ends in waking.

He says it again to the church in Thessalonica, so they will "not grieve like the rest, who are without hope." "For since we believe that Jesus died and rose again, we also believe that God will bring with Jesus those who have fallen asleep in Him" (1 Thessalonians 4:13–14, BSB). The grief is real. It is not a grief without hope.

The ones I have lost who were in Christ are not gone for good. They are sleeping. And the Lord who died and rose will bring them with him.

That is why one more verse may be the best match for the whole of David's prayer. "So it is said," Paul writes: "Wake up, O sleeper, rise up from the dead, and Christ will shine on you" (Ephesians 5:14, BSB). David prays, "Give light to my eyes, lest I sleep." Paul answers that Christ himself shines on the sleeper, and wakes him.

The light David asked for has a name. The sleep he feared is not the last word. That is the hope I am holding for the ones I have lost, and for myself, when my own turn comes.

David already knew something of that turn. After the complaint comes the trust: "But I have trusted in Your loving devotion; my heart will rejoice in Your salvation" (Psalm 13:5, BSB). And then the song I want to be able to sing, last of the Mohicans or not—not because the list got shorter, but because He has been good:

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>I will sing to the LORD, for He has been good to me.</em><br />
Psalm 13:6 (BSB)</p>
