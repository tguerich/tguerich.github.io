A Reflection on Psalm 20
{: .study-subtitle}

Tony Guerich · November 20, 2026
{: .study-byline}

This psalm is a prayer said over the king before he goes to battle.

It is a “day of trouble,” and the people gather around him with a blessing:

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>May the LORD answer you in the day of trouble;</em><br />
<em>may the name of the God of Jacob protect you.</em><br />
Psalm 20:1 (BSB)</p>

They ask for help from the sanctuary, and for the desires of his heart.

Then they lift a banner, not in the king’s name, but “in the name of our God” (Psalm 20:5, BSB).

And then a single voice speaks, and the psalm turns:

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>Now I know that the LORD saves His anointed;</em><br />
<em>He answers him from His holy heaven</em><br />
<em>with the saving power of His right hand.</em><br />
Psalm 20:6 (BSB)</p>

The English says “saves.”

It sounds like a hope still under way.

The Hebrew says more.

The verb is <span lang="he">הוֹשִׁיעַ</span> (<em>hoshia</em>), “has saved.”<sup id="fnref1"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup>

It is spoken as already done, even though the battle has not yet come.

A promise so sure it can be told in the past tense. Some call that the prophetic perfect: the future spoken as already finished.

“Now I know.” Not later, when the fighting is over. Now, while the trouble is still the day they are in.

And right after “has saved,” the Hebrew scribes marked a small vertical stroke, a brief pause, so the ear stops on that word before it hears who does the saving: the LORD.

Whom has He saved?

His anointed.

The word is <span lang="he">מְשִׁיחוֹ</span> (<em>meshicho</em>), “His Messiah.”

For the people who first sang this, it was their king, the one anointed with oil, set apart as the LORD’s own, and sent to stand for them.

But when this psalm was put into Greek, long before the first Christmas, Greek-speaking readers met a stunning line:

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><span lang="grc">νῦν ἔγνων ὅτι ἔσωσεν κύριος τὸν χριστὸν αὐτοῦ</span><br />
<em>“Now I know that the Lord has saved his Christ.”</em><br />
<em>(Brenton, 1851)</em><sup id="fnref2"><a href="#fn2">2</a></sup></p>

They did not read, “May the Lord save His Christ.”

They read that He already had.

The Lord has saved His Christ.

That is the same title the New Testament gives to Jesus.

And the Hebrew verb shares its root with Jesus’ own name, Yeshua, and with the cry Hosanna, “Save!”

The psalm even ends with that same word, <span lang="he">הוֹשִׁיעָה</span> (<em>hoshiah</em>), “Save!”:

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>O LORD, save the king.</em><br />
<em>Answer us on the day we call.</em><br />
Psalm 20:9 (BSB)</p>

I almost read right past it.

“Now I know that the LORD saves His anointed.”

Then I slowed down. In Hebrew, the LORD <em>has saved</em> His Messiah. Already done, before the battle even starts.

So who does the saving? The LORD.

And whom does He save? His Messiah, His Christ.

Then it hit me. The name Jesus, Yeshua in Hebrew, means “the LORD saves.”<sup id="fnref3"><a href="#fn3">3</a></sup>

The psalm was spelling His name before He was born.

At the cross they sneered, “Let Him save Himself.” He didn’t.

The Father saved Him, just as the psalm said: through death, not from it.

Now I know.

At the cross we hear it again:

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>The people stood watching, and the rulers sneered at Him, saying, “He saved others; let Him save Himself if He is the Christ of God, the Chosen One.”</em><br />
Luke 23:35 (BSB)</p>

They took His hanging there as proof that God had not saved Him.

If He were the Christ of God, they thought, He would save Himself.

The mockers were not wrong that He is the Christ of God.

They were wrong about what saving would look like.

“But God raised Him from the dead, releasing Him from the agony of death, because it was impossible for Him to be held in its clutches” (Acts 2:24, BSB).

Death could not hold Him.

And the answer came, as the psalm said it would, “from His holy heaven.”

That is Easter, already heard in Psalm 20.

The LORD has saved His Messiah.

The king’s day of trouble has become the cross, and the empty tomb is the answer from heaven.

We still call.
