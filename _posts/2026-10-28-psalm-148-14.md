## Verse 14: The Hebrew

Psalm 148 calls everything in heaven and earth to praise the LORD. Verse 14 gives the reason: God has acted for one particular people, and that people is “near to him.”

### Key words

- **<span lang="he" dir="rtl">וַיָּרֶם קֶרֶן</span>**, “he raised a horn.” A horn is the animal’s weapon and its crown, so it stands for strength and dignity. Raising a horn means God has given his people strength and lifted their heads. Hannah’s song uses the same picture: “He will give strength to his king, and exalt the horn of his anointed.” (1 Samuel 2:10, WEB). The verb is a narrative past: the psalmist speaks of something God has already done.[^4]
- **<span lang="he" dir="rtl">חֲסִידָיו</span>**, “his faithful ones,” the plural of <span lang="he" dir="rtl">חָסִיד</span>. It is built on the same root as <span lang="he" dir="rtl">חֶסֶד</span>, God’s steadfast covenant love. The people who receive that love and answer it with loyalty are his faithful ones.
- **<span lang="he" dir="rtl">קְרֹבוֹ</span>**, “his near one,” the ordinary adjective <span lang="he" dir="rtl">קָרוֹב</span>, “near,” with “his.” The same adjective with a suffix describes the priests in Leviticus 10:3, <span lang="he" dir="rtl">בִּקְרֹבַי</span>, “among those near me.” In Psalm 148 it is applied to the whole people of Israel.[^5]

## Septuagint[^6]

> καὶ ὑψώσει κέρας λαοῦ αὐτοῦ ὕμνος πᾶσι τοῖς ὁσίοις αὐτοῦ τοῖς υἱοῖς Ισραηλ λαῷ ἐγγίζοντι αὐτῷ
>
> *“And he will exalt the horn of his people; a hymn for all his holy ones, for the sons of Israel, a people drawing near to him.”* <span class="src">(literal)</span>
{: .scripture}

- **ὑψώσει**, future: “he will exalt.” Where the Hebrew looks back on what God has done, the Greek reads it as a promise. The same form returns in James 4:10.
- **κέρας λαοῦ αὐτοῦ**, “the horn *of* his people,” a genitive where the Hebrew has “for his people.” The WEB’s “horn of his people” follows the same sense.
- **ὁσίοις** translates <span lang="he" dir="rtl">חֲסִידָיו</span>, and **ὕμνος** translates <span lang="he" dir="rtl">תְּהִלָּה</span>.
- **λαῷ ἐγγίζοντι αὐτῷ**: ἐγγίζοντι is the present active participle of ἐγγίζω. The translator turned the Hebrew adjective “near” into a verb of motion: “a people *drawing near* to him.” Nearness becomes something the people keep doing, not only a place where they stand.
- This participle is the Septuagint’s word for priests: ἐν τοῖς ἐγγίζουσίν μοι in Leviticus 10:3, τοῖς ἐγγίζουσι πρός με in Ezekiel 43:19. In Psalm 148:14 the whole people is described in priestly language.

## Old Testament Background: Drawing Near[^7]

### The priests who are near

- **Leviticus 10:3.** Right after the death of Nadab and Abihu: “I will show myself holy to those who come near me.” Hebrew <span lang="he" dir="rtl">בִּקְרֹבַי אֶקָּדֵשׁ</span>, the same adjective as in Psalm 148:14; Greek ἐν τοῖς ἐγγίζουσίν μοι ἁγιασθήσομαι. Nearness to God is a privilege, and it is holy ground: the Holy One must be treated as holy.
- **Leviticus 9:7.** Moses tells Aaron, “Draw near to the altar.” The Hebrew verb is <span lang="he" dir="rtl">קְרַב</span>; the Greek is πρόσελθε, from προσέρχομαι, the verb Hebrews will use.
- **Numbers 16:5.** In the Korah rebellion, God “will cause him to come near to him. Even him whom he shall choose, he will cause to come near to him.” The Hebrew uses the causative of <span lang="he" dir="rtl">קָרַב</span> twice (<span lang="he" dir="rtl">וְהִקְרִיב</span> … <span lang="he" dir="rtl">יַקְרִיב</span>); the Greek has προσηγάγετο. Nearness is God’s to give.
- **Ezekiel 42:13; 43:19.** “the priests who are near to Yahweh,” <span lang="he" dir="rtl">אֲשֶׁר־קְרוֹבִים לַיהֹוָה</span>; and the sons of Zadok “who are near to me,” <span lang="he" dir="rtl">הַקְּרֹבִים אֵלַי</span>. The Greek has οἱ ἐγγίζοντες πρὸς κύριον and τοῖς ἐγγίζουσι πρός με.

### Israel’s nearness, and God’s

- **Exodus 19:6.** “you shall be to me a kingdom of priests, and a holy nation” (Greek βασίλειον ἱεράτευμα). The verse does not use the “near” words, but it helps explain how a psalm can call the whole nation near.
- **Deuteronomy 4:7.** “For what great nation is there, that has a god so near to them, as Yahweh our God is whenever we call on him?” Hebrew <span lang="he" dir="rtl">אֱלֹהִים קְרֹבִים אֵלָיו</span>; Greek θεὸς ἐγγίζων αὐτοῖς. Here God is the one who comes near, with the same participle.
- **Psalm 34:18** (Hebrew 34:19; Greek 33:19). “Yahweh is near to those who have a broken heart.” Hebrew <span lang="he" dir="rtl">קָרוֹב יְהֹוָה לְנִשְׁבְּרֵי־לֵב</span>; Greek ἐγγὺς κύριος τοῖς συντετριμμένοις τὴν καρδίαν.
- **Psalm 145:18** (Greek 144:18). “Yahweh is near to all those who call on him, to all who call on him in truth.” Hebrew <span lang="he" dir="rtl">קָרוֹב יְהֹוָה לְכׇל־קֹרְאָיו</span>; Greek ἐγγὺς κύριος πᾶσιν τοῖς ἐπικαλουμένοις αὐτόν. The circle is already wide: *all* who call.
- **Isaiah 57:19.** “Peace, peace, to him who is far off and to him who is near.” Hebrew <span lang="he" dir="rtl">שָׁלוֹם שָׁלוֹם לָרָחוֹק וְלַקָּרוֹב</span>; Greek εἰρήνην ἐπ᾿ εἰρήνην τοῖς μακρὰν καὶ τοῖς ἐγγὺς οὖσιν.

## The New Testament Trace[^8]

### James 4:8: the invitation in plain words

> <sup class="vnum">8</sup>ἐγγίσατε τῷ θεῷ, καὶ ἐγγιεῖ ὑμῖν.
>
> <sup class="vnum">8</sup>*Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you.*[^9]
{: .scripture}

The verb is ἐγγίζω, the Septuagint’s priestly verb from Psalm 148:14 and Leviticus 10:3, and here it is a command to ordinary believers. The call to holiness remains: “Cleanse your hands, you sinners; and purify your hearts, you double-minded.” The God who draws near in Deuteronomy 4:7 (ἐγγίζων) answers the one who draws near to him.

The setting is the one traced in the Psalm 147:6 study:

> <sup class="vnum">6</sup>Ὁ θεὸς ὑπερηφάνοις ἀντιτάσσεται ταπεινοῖς δὲ δίδωσιν χάριν.
>
> <sup class="vnum">6</sup>*“God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble.”*
>
> <sup class="vnum">10</sup>ταπεινώθητε ἐνώπιον κυρίου, καὶ ὑψώσει ὑμᾶς.
>
> <sup class="vnum">10</sup>*Humble yourselves in the sight of the Lord, and he will exalt you.*
{: .scripture}

1 Peter 5:5–6 has the same pairing: the same quotation, then Ταπεινώθητε οὖν … ἵνα ὑμᾶς ὑψώσῃ. Drawing near sits between two statements about humility, and it ends with the same verb and form as Psalm 148:14 in the Septuagint: **ὑψώσει**, “he will exalt.” Whether James had the psalm in mind cannot be shown, but the two agree: God lifts up those who come near to him humbly.

### Hebrews 7:19: a better hope

> <sup class="vnum">19</sup>ἐπεισαγωγὴ δὲ κρείττονος ἐλπίδος, δι’ ἧς ἐγγίζομεν τῷ θεῷ.
>
> <sup class="vnum">19</sup>*a bringing in of a better hope, through which we draw near to God.*
{: .scripture}

Again ἐγγίζω, now with “we” as the subject. “the law made nothing perfect,” but the hope that Christ’s priesthood brings in is the way God’s people now draw near.

### Ephesians 2:13, 17: the far off made near

> <sup class="vnum">13</sup>νυνὶ δὲ ἐν Χριστῷ Ἰησοῦ ὑμεῖς οἵ ποτε ὄντες μακρὰν ἐγενήθητε ἐγγὺς ἐν τῷ αἵματι τοῦ Χριστοῦ.
>
> <sup class="vnum">13</sup>*But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off are made near in the blood of Christ.*
>
> <sup class="vnum">17</sup>καὶ ἐλθὼν εὐηγγελίσατο εἰρήνην ὑμῖν τοῖς μακρὰν καὶ εἰρήνην τοῖς ἐγγύς·
>
> <sup class="vnum">17</sup>*He came and preached peace to you who were far off and to those who were near.*[^10]
{: .scripture}

Compare Isaiah 57:19 in the Septuagint:

<tr><th scope="row">Isaiah 57:19</th><td class="greek" lang="grc">εἰρήνην ἐπ᾿ εἰρήνην <strong>τοῖς μακρὰν</strong> καὶ <strong>τοῖς ἐγγὺς</strong> οὖσιν</td></tr>
<tr><th scope="row">Ephesians 2:17</th><td class="greek" lang="grc">εἰρήνην ὑμῖν <strong>τοῖς μακρὰν</strong> καὶ εἰρήνην <strong>τοῖς ἐγγύς</strong></td></tr>

Paul’s words line up closely with Isaiah’s: peace, then τοῖς μακρὰν, then τοῖς ἐγγύς. He repeats εἰρήνην, which is closer to the Hebrew’s doubled <span lang="he" dir="rtl">שָׁלוֹם</span> than to the Greek’s εἰρήνην ἐπ᾿ εἰρήνην. Ephesians 2:17 clearly echoes Isaiah 57:19. “Those who were near” are Israel, the <span lang="he" dir="rtl">עַם קְרֹבוֹ</span> of our psalm; “you who were far off” are the Gentiles. Paul does not set Israel’s nearness aside. He shows the far-off being brought into it “in the blood of Christ.”[^11]

### Hebrews 4:16 and 10:22: a different verb, the same approach

> <sup class="vnum">16</sup>προσερχώμεθα οὖν μετὰ παρρησίας τῷ θρόνῳ τῆς χάριτος…
>
> <sup class="vnum">16</sup>*Let us therefore draw near with boldness to the throne of grace…*
>
> <sup class="vnum">22</sup>προσερχώμεθα μετὰ ἀληθινῆς καρδίας ἐν πληροφορίᾳ πίστεως…
>
> <sup class="vnum">22</sup>*let’s draw near with a true heart in fullness of faith…*
{: .scripture}

Both verses use **προσέρχομαι**, not ἐγγίζω. It too is a Septuagint word for priestly approach: Moses tells Aaron πρόσελθε πρὸς τὸ θυσιαστήριον, “draw near to the altar” (Leviticus 9:7). Hebrews uses it again in 7:25, τοὺς προσερχομένους δι’ αὐτοῦ τῷ θεῷ, “those who draw near to God through him.” The whole congregation is invited to come where only priests once could, “having our hearts sprinkled from an evil conscience, and having our body washed with pure water.”

### The horn: Luke 1:69

> <sup class="vnum">69</sup>καὶ ἤγειρεν κέρας σωτηρίας ἡμῖν ἐν οἴκῳ Δαυὶδ παιδὸς αὐτοῦ,
>
> <sup class="vnum">69</sup>*and has raised up a horn of salvation for us in the house of his servant David*
{: .scripture}

Zechariah’s song shares κέρας and the idea of raising a horn with Psalm 148:14 (ὑψώσει κέρας), though the verbs differ: Luke has ἤγειρεν, the psalm ὑψώσει. The exact phrase κέρας σωτηρίας is found in the Septuagint at Psalm 17:3 (= 18:2) and 2 Kingdoms 22:3; a horn for David appears in Psalm 131:17 (ἐξανατελῶ κέρας τῷ Δαυιδ) and in Hannah’s ὑψώσει κέρας χριστοῦ αὐτοῦ (1 Kingdoms 2:10). Psalm 148:14 belongs to this family of texts, not as Luke’s direct source. Read together, the psalm praises God for raising a horn for his people, and Zechariah names that horn: a Savior from David’s house.

### Drawing near to God elsewhere in the New Testament

Most uses of **ἐγγίζω** concern the kingdom, a day, an hour, or a place drawing near (for example Mark 1:15; Romans 13:12; Hebrews 10:25; James 5:8). Only two passages use it of people drawing near to God: James 4:8 (twice: we to him, he to us) and Hebrews 7:19.[^12]

**ἐγγύς** in this sense:

- **Ephesians 2:13, 17**: the far off made near, and peace to those near.
- **Philippians 4:5**, ὁ κύριος ἐγγύς, “The Lord is at hand.” This may mean near in time, near in presence, or both.
- **Romans 10:8**, Ἐγγύς σου τὸ ῥῆμά ἐστιν, “The word is near you.” This is the nearness of the message of faith (drawing on Deuteronomy 30:14, ἔστιν σου ἐγγὺς τὸ ῥῆμα), not of God himself.
- **Acts 17:27** says it with other words: God is οὐ μακρὰν ἀπὸ ἑνὸς ἑκάστου ἡμῶν, “not far from each one of us.”

## Near and Far: The Promise to Abraham

The promise to Abraham had the nations in view from the start: “All the families of the earth will be blessed through you.” (Genesis 12:3, WEB). The Hebrew is <span lang="he" dir="rtl">וְנִבְרְכוּ בְךָ כֹּל מִשְׁפְּחֹת הָאֲדָמָה</span>; the Septuagint, ἐνευλογηθήσονται ἐν σοὶ πᾶσαι αἱ φυλαὶ τῆς γῆς. Israel, the near people of Psalm 148:14, was the bearer of that promise.[^13]

Ephesians 2 describes the Gentiles as standing outside it: “alienated from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers from the covenants of the promise, having no hope and without God in the world” (2:12; ξένοι τῶν διαθηκῶν τῆς ἐπαγγελίας). The next verse is the turn: “But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off are made near in the blood of Christ.” The far-off are not brought near to some other people. They are brought into the promise Israel carried.

Paul says the same in Galatians:

> *The Scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the Gentiles by faith, preached the Good News beforehand to Abraham, saying, “In you all the nations will be blessed.”* <span class="src">(Galatians 3:8, WEB)</span>
{: .scripture}

A few verses later he names the purpose: “that the blessing of Abraham might come on the Gentiles through Christ Jesus” (3:14). The near and the far in Psalm 148, Isaiah 57, and Ephesians 2 are the Abrahamic promise unfolding: through Abraham’s family, all the families of the earth.[^14]

## Closing Thought

Psalm 148 calls angels, stars, seas, mountains, kings, and children to praise the LORD, and comes to rest on one people: Israel, “a people near to him.” That nearness was a gift, never an achievement. God chose whom he would bring near (Numbers 16:5), and it was holy ground (Leviticus 10:3). Yet the Septuagint already pictures this people as *drawing near*, ἐγγίζοντι, a nation doing what priests did.

The New Testament does not erase that nearness. It opens the door, as God promised Abraham it would. Through Christ’s blood, those “far off” are “made near” (Ephesians 2:13). Through a better hope, “we draw near to God” (Hebrews 7:19). We “draw near with boldness to the throne of grace” (Hebrews 4:16). James puts it in one sentence anyone can obey: “Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you.” He sets it between grace to the humble and the promise that God will lift up those who humble themselves. The God who raised a horn for his near people still lifts up all who come to him on those terms.
