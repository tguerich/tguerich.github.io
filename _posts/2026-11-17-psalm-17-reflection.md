A Reflection on Psalm 17
{: .study-subtitle}

Tony Guerich · November 17, 2026
{: .study-byline}

Psalm 17 is a prayer of David, and it opens with a cry. In the Berean Standard Bible it reads:

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>Hear, O LORD, my righteous plea; listen to my cry. Give ear to my prayer— it comes from lips free of deceit.</em><br />
Psalm 17:1 (BSB)</p>

In the Hebrew, the verse is:<sup id="fnref1"><a href="#fn1">1</a></sup>

<p dir="rtl" lang="he" style="font-size:1.4em">תְּפִלָּ֗ה לְדָ֫וִ֥ד<br />
שִׁמְעָ֤ה יְהֹוָ֨ה ׀ צֶ֗דֶק הַקְשִׁ֥יבָה רִנָּתִ֗י<br />
הַאֲזִ֥ינָה תְפִלָּתִ֑י בְּ֝לֹ֗א שִׂפְתֵ֥י מִרְמָֽה׃</p>

I was looking at that first cry, and one mark stopped me. Over the word <span lang="he">צֶ֗דֶק</span> there is a revia, what the grammars call rebia' gadol (literally "great resting," a big pause mark). I keep saying my Hebrew is terrible. But the mark is doing real work. William Barrick, in <em><a href="https://drbarrick.org/files/papers/other/HebrewAccentsrev.pdf">The Masoretic Hebrew Accents in Translation and Interpretation</a></em>, says that rebia' gadol most often marks the quarter points of a verse, but "sometimes it identifies a key word or topic for a verse or section of verse." After the Name there is also a little vertical stroke, a pause in the line. The accent marks read it in beats: Hear, O LORD — Righteousness!

So I tried a more literal paraphrase, and let the word stand alone:

<p style="margin-left:36pt"><em>Hear, O LORD —</em><br />
<em>Righteousness!</em><br />
<em>Attend to my cry;</em><br />
<em>give ear to my prayer,</em><br />
<em>from lips without deceit.</em></p>

The English has smoothed it into "my righteous plea."<sup id="fnref2"><a href="#fn2">2</a></sup> The Hebrew just cries the bare word.

David goes on, and he is not shy about it. "You have tried my heart; You have visited me in the night. You have tested me and found no evil; I have resolved not to sin with my mouth" (Psalm 17:3, BSB). "As for the deeds of men — by the word of Your lips I have avoided the ways of the violent. My steps have held to Your paths; my feet have not slipped" (Psalm 17:4–5, BSB).

I know that prayer. And I know what I cannot say.

<p style="text-align:center">Personally I wouldn't make those claims before God.</p>

That is the honest line. It belongs with what I already had to say in "<a href="/psalm-studies/psalm-15-reflection/">No One</a>," a reflection on Psalm 15. Who may dwell on the holy mountain? No one. Not me. When David cries "Righteousness!", the only safe way for me to cry it is for God's righteousness, not my own.

Paul knew that cry. He wanted to "be found in Him, not having my own righteousness from the law, but that which is through faith in Christ, the righteousness from God on the basis of faith" (Philippians 3:9, BSB). And this: "God made Him who knew no sin to be sin on our behalf, so that in Him we might become the righteousness of God" (2 Corinthians 5:21, BSB).

I cannot stand before God on my own record. The good news is that I do not have to. The psalm does not end with David's claim. It ends with a looking: "As for me, I will behold Your face in righteousness; when I awake, I will be satisfied in Your presence" (Psalm 17:15, BSB). That is the righteousness I can cry, and the waking I am waiting for.

## Notes

<sup id="fn1"><a href="#fnref1">1</a></sup> The Hebrew text is from the Miqra according to the Masorah on Sefaria.

<sup id="fn2"><a href="#fnref2">2</a></sup> "Reading the Bible in translation is like kissing your bride through a veil." William Barrick quotes that line from the poet Haim Nachman Bialik. The English kisses "my righteous plea" through the cloth. The Hebrew just cries the bare word: Righteousness!
